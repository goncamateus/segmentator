\section{Auditing a chain: proof, and the refusal to claim it}
\label{sec:optimizer}

A chain tuned by hand is a ratchet. A stage goes in to fix what is on screen, a
slider moves, another stage goes on top of it, and nothing is ever taken back
out. Three edits later the stage that was load-bearing contributes nothing, and
nobody can tell by looking --- removing it to find out means losing the chain
that currently works. So the stage stays, and the next one is added on top.

The editor can analyse a chain and propose a shorter one that produces the same
output. What makes this worth a section is not that it finds redundancy, but that
it distinguishes two kinds of finding that are usually presented as one, and
refuses to dress the weaker kind as the stronger.

\subsection{What can be proved}

The first \OptProved{} rows of Table~\ref{tab:findings} are sound by
construction, and none of them needed a single frame of video to establish.

\texttt{morphology} at position~8 has \texttt{iterations: 0}. A stage
parameterised into being a passthrough is a passthrough for every possible input,
and the parameter values that do this are declared on the stage class rather than
guessed at by the analysis.

\texttt{canny} at position~4 has its output discarded: the \texttt{apply\_mask}
below it reads the source frame and a published mask, not the working image, so
whatever \texttt{canny} produced is overwritten unread. That is a dataflow
argument over the config, and it holds no matter what the pixels are. The same
walk finds the second \texttt{gray} at position~1, applied to an image that is
already single-channel.

The three per-pixel stages at positions 9--11 collapse into one lookup table, and
this is the strongest claim in the table: the fused table is built by running the
three operators over all 256 possible input values and recording the result, so
the replacement is verified exhaustively over the whole input domain rather than
argued about. It is not ``these look equivalent''; it is ``there is no input on which
they differ.''

\subsection{What cannot}

The remaining \OptSampled{} rows come from a different procedure: drop the stage,
re-run the chain over \OptFrames{} sampled frames, and compare everything the
sinks can observe. When nothing differs, the only honest statement is
\emph{no counterexample was found in \OptFrames{} frames}, and that is exactly
what the tool reports. It is falsification, not proof.

The sample size is not a detail. On the chain this analysis was first built
against, one stage looked redundant at four sampled frames and was demonstrably
load-bearing at eight. The tool therefore refuses to answer below a floor of
eight frames rather than answer badly, and a chain that holds state is sampled
over a contiguous run rather than scattered frames --- a background model handed
six frames from six different minutes has not seen the history its output
depends on.

The last row of the table is the argument for all of this. The optimizer proposes
dropping \texttt{mean\_background} --- the fixed background subtraction that is
the entire reason this recipe exists --- because on all \OptFrames{} sampled
frames the pixels the sink received were identical with it and without it. The
finding is not a bug. It is a true and correctly-labelled statement about sixteen
frames, and it is useless as advice. A reader of that row knows in one second
what no amount of sampling would establish; the tool's job is to hand them the
row with an honest label on it, not to pretend the label says something else.

Hence the last rule: nothing here is ever applied automatically. The analysis
proposes, a human accepts, and accepting several findings at once re-checks them
together --- because findings that each hold alone do not necessarily hold in
combination. The measured saving attached to each row comes from timing every
stage once inside a single interleaved pass rather than timing whole candidate
chains against each other, which does not survive contact with a warm cache: the
same chain over the same frames measured 77~ms cold and 40~ms after the search
had been hammering those arrays for fifteen seconds, a drift far larger than most
of the stages being priced.
