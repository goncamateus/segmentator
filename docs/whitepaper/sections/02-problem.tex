\section{The problem}

This project began with twenty-seven minutes of thermal video: $320\times240$
pixels, 15~frames per second, a fixed camera, and somewhere in the frame a gas
plume that had to be pulled out of it.

A plume is an awkward thing to segment. It has no edges to detect, because its
boundary is where it stops being denser than the air behind it. It has no shape
to match, because it has a new shape in every frame. It has no colour to key on,
because a thermal sensor gives one channel and the plume sits a few grey levels
above the background it drifts across. What it does have is that it moves while
the rest of the scene does not, and that it changes the statistics of the pixels
it passes over. Every method worth trying is a way of exploiting one of those two
facts, and which combination works is not something anyone can tell you from a
description of the footage.

The first working answer was a script, and it worked: mask the region of interest,
average the first sixty frames into a fixed background, subtract it, threshold
twice to drop the noise floor and clip the reflections. \LegacyLOC{}~lines across
\LegacyModules{} modules, and a plume on the screen.

The trouble was everything that came after it. Would an adaptive subtractor beat
the fixed mean once the sun moved? What does the plume look like if the contrast
is lifted before subtraction rather than after? How many objects does the frame
break into if the morphological opening uses a $5\times5$ kernel instead of
$3\times3$? Every one of those questions is a question about the footage, and
every one of them was answered by editing a Python file that also owned frame
decoding, the region mask and the video writer. The recipe and the machinery were
the same code, so exploring the first meant risking the second --- and comparing
two answers meant either keeping two copies of the script or losing the first one
to get the second.

That is not a gas problem. It is the shape of most of this kind of work: the
machinery is nearly constant --- read frames in order, apply operators in
sequence, write out images and measurements --- while the recipe changes several
times an hour for as long as anyone is still looking at the footage. The
predecessor had them fused, which is why it had a ceiling. Everything in this
document follows from separating them, and from one decision about which side of
the line the recipe belongs on: it is data, not code.
