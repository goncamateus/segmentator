\section{What this replaced, and what it does not replace}

\subsection{The script it grew out of}

Figure~\ref{fig:diff} puts one change side by side in both architectures:
replacing the fixed mean background with OpenCV's adaptive MOG2 subtractor. It is
an ordinary change --- the first thing anyone would try when a scene's lighting
drifts --- and it is the kind of change that happens several times in an
afternoon of tuning.

In the predecessor it is \LegacyChanged{} changed lines across \LegacyFiles{}
files, and one module deleted outright: a field goes out of the config dataclass
and three come in, an import disappears, the constructor changes shape, and the
loop body changes because the new subtractor has a different interface from the
old one. Nothing there is difficult. All of it is the kind of edit that has to be
reviewed, can be got wrong, and cannot be undone by pressing undo once.

In a config it is one line.

The comparison that would be dishonest to leave out is the other one. The
predecessor is \LegacyLOC{} lines; the engine described here is \RepoLOC{}, some
thirty times larger. Nothing about this approach makes less code. What it does is
put the code on the other side of a line: the \RepoLOC{} lines are written once
and then edited rarely, while the thing that changes several times an hour became
a file that cannot break frame decoding, cannot corrupt the encoder, and can be
kept, diffed, named and re-run a month later. The predecessor's \LegacyLOC{}
lines could express exactly one recipe. The registry currently holds
\NumStages{} stages, \NumSources{} sources and \NumSinks{} sinks, and the number
of recipes expressible from them is not a number worth writing down.

\subsection{And why not a learned model}

The obvious question about any classical segmentation pipeline in 2026 is why it
is not a neural network. Three answers, in the order they actually bind.

\paragraph{Cost of entry.} A supervised model needs labelled plume masks, and
none exist for this footage. Producing them means a person drawing boundaries on
a soft-edged, semi-transparent cloud across thousands of frames, and agreeing
with themselves about where the plume ends --- a labelling task with genuine
ambiguity in it, not a tedious-but-obvious one. The classical chain needs no
labels at all and ran on day one. For a question whose answer is worth having
this week, that difference decides it.

\paragraph{This is how the labels would get made.} The output of these recipes is
already the shape a training set wants: the crops sink cuts every detected object
out of the frame and writes it as an image, the CSV sink writes one row per object
with its geometry, and the JSON sink writes per-frame scalars. A tuned classical
chain over a season of footage is a bootstrap labelling pass, to be corrected by a
human rather than produced by one. That is a use for this tool alongside a learned
model, not against it, and it is the concrete route from here to one
(§\ref{sec:next}).

\paragraph{The failures are legible.} Every parameter here means something
physical --- a threshold in grey levels, a kernel in pixels, a background over
$N$ frames --- and every intermediate image can be looked at. When a chain starts
producing nonsense, §\ref{sec:optimizer}'s per-stage view says which stage, and
the fix is a number. When a trained model starts producing nonsense on footage
that drifted away from its training set, the fix is a retraining job and a new
dataset. Neither is better in general; they fail differently, and the second
failure mode is worse to be holding at three in the morning.

None of that is an argument that this beats a learned model at segmenting a
plume. Given labelled data and a GPU, a modern segmentation network would very
likely be more accurate than anything in this document, and this document
contains no accuracy measurement with which to argue otherwise. The claim is
narrower and, for an industrial reader, more useful: this runs today, on footage
nobody has labelled, on a CPU, at more than real time, and it tells you what it
did.
